SETTING UP YOUR WORM BIN — TRANSCRIPT
Urban Worm Ranching

Now that we've looked at the basic parts of a worm bin, let's put them together.

As we go, pay attention not only to what we're adding, but why each part matters. A healthy worm bin is a living system, so the goal isn't to follow a perfect recipe. It's to create good starting conditions and then learn to observe how the system responds.

PREPARE THE CONTAINERS

There are plenty of ready-made worm bin kits available, but a simple two-bin stacking system is one of the most economical and approachable ways to get started. It uses basic materials, doesn't take up much space, and gives us the airflow and drainage we're looking for.

Start with your containers. Your worm bin needs airflow, so you'll need ventilation holes near the upper part of the inner bin. You'll also want drainage holes in the bottom in case excess moisture collects.

If you're using a nested-bin setup like this one, spacers between the containers help create room underneath for drainage while keeping the bins from fitting too tightly together.

The exact container you use may vary depending on your space. What's important is that it's manageable for you and provides the airflow and drainage the system needs.

ADD BEDDING

Next, add about four to six inches of bedding.

Shredded cardboard and paper are inexpensive options, and you may already have them at home. Other dry materials can work as well. You'll also want a small amount of grit, such as finely crushed eggshells or a little soil, which helps support the worms' digestive process.

You don't need every material on this list. Think about the function each material serves and use appropriate materials that are practical for you.

ADD MOISTURE

Once the bedding is in place, add moisture gradually.

You're aiming for bedding that is evenly moist, but not saturated. Add a little water, mix the bedding, and check it before adding more.

This is one of those places where observation matters more than following an exact measurement. Conditions will vary depending on your bedding, container, and environment.

ADD WORMS

Once the bedding environment is ready, it's time to add your worms.

Gently place them onto the bedding and give them some time to move into their new environment. You can add a light layer of bedding over them rather than burying them deeply.

Notice that we're adding the worms only after we've established their habitat. We're building the environment first and introducing the living part of the system second.

FEED WORMS

Now you can begin feeding.

Worms can process a variety of suitable materials, including many fruit and vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, crushed eggshells, and some paper-based materials. There are also foods you'll want to avoid, which you can see here.

For a new bin, resist the temptation to add everything you've saved. Start with a modest amount of food and observe how quickly it disappears. You can always add more later.

That habit—feed, observe, and adjust—is much easier than trying to correct an overfed bin.

OBSERVE AND MAINTAIN

From here, your most important tool is observation.

Check the moisture, notice how quickly food is being consumed, and pay attention to changes in odor. If something seems wrong, make a small adjustment based on what you're observing rather than changing several things at once.

If you're using this two-bin system, also remember to check the lower container for liquid that has drained from the worm bin. This liquid is called leachate. Don't allow it to accumulate indefinitely; check the lower bin regularly and drain it as needed. If you want to use the leachate around garden plants, it can be diluted with approximately ten parts water to one part leachate.

A worm bin doesn't operate on a rigid schedule. Conditions change, so let the bin tell you what it needs.

Over the next few months, the worms will transform these materials into castings that you can harvest and use to support healthy soil and plants.
